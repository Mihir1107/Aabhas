# Presentation package: every number, with the condition it was measured under

**Who this is for.** The four of you building the six SIH slides. You have not
read the code and you should not have to. Everything you might put on a slide is
below, with the exact condition attached.

**The one rule.** Copy the number AND its condition. Every number in this file
has a condition line next to it. If you drop the condition, a judge will ask for
it and we will not have it on the slide.

**Vocabulary, once.** *Yield loss* is the percentage of good parts we wrongly
reject. *Escape rate* is the percentage of genuinely defective parts we wrongly
pass. *Recall* is the opposite of escape rate: the percentage of defective parts
we catch. *Test lots* means production batches the model never saw during
training. *Lot* means one manufacturing batch of 500 components.

Dataset `dataset-v1.2`. Every number below was regenerated by the full pipeline
after the final pre-submission audit (branch `fix/final-review`). The
documentation site, <https://mihir1107.github.io/Aabhas/>, is generated from the
same results and carries the same numbers.

## Four number-consistency traps

**Trap 1. Type VI has two correct answers, and they are very different.**
Type VI is the "whole lot shifted" trap: a batch where the manufacturing process
drifted, so every part in it looks unusual against history but normal against
its own batch. The headline is that static limits scrap them and dynamic limits
do not. The size of that effect depends entirely on where you set the threshold:

| Condition | Static PAT flags | Dynamic PAT flags |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed 6 sigma (the AEC-Q001 standard multiplier) | **11.93%** (358 of 3,000 parts) | **0.00%** (0 parts) |
| Matched to a 7% yield-loss budget | 59.07% (1,772 parts) | 5.73% (172 parts) |

**Use the 6 sigma row.** It is the standard's own operating point, it needs no
arbitrary budget, and it is the cleaner contrast (358 versus 0).

**Trap 2. C1 and L1b are the same detector but report slightly different
scores.** In `ablation_cumulative.csv`, rung C1 shows PR-AUC 0.258 and AUROC
0.833. In `ablation.csv`, rung L1b (the same DPAT detector) shows 0.259 and
0.841. This is not an error. C1 is defined as "L0 plus L1", so it also includes
the four genuinely good parts that fall outside datasheet limits by chance, and
those sit at the top of the combined ranking. Recall is identical (54.33%).
If you show a cumulative ladder, use the C-numbers throughout. If you show a
head-to-head method table, use the L-numbers throughout. Do not mix.

**Trap 3. The estimator table in the old v1.0 README does not reproduce.**
Those figures were computed on a 20-lot trial run, not on the frozen 240-lot
dataset. Use the validator's output or the docs site.

**Trap 4. The ladder recall and the decision-layer recall are different
numbers.** The ladder (92.7%) is measured at a 7% yield-loss budget. The six
decision tiers an inspector sees hold parts at a stricter 2% budget, and hold
92.0% of test-lot defects. Both are correct; say which one you mean.

Say "at identical yield loss" out loud. It is the part that makes the comparison

## 2. Module B prediction error (MAE)

Mean Absolute Error, in physical units: the average gap between the predicted
168 hour value and the actual one.

| Parameter | MAE, all parts | MAE, good parts | MAE, defective parts | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quiescent current (Iddq) | 0.610 uA | 0.481 uA | 7.753 uA | 16.1x |
| Input leakage | 1.298 nA | 1.110 nA | 11.642 nA | 10.5x |
| Propagation delay | 0.067 ns | 0.062 ns | 0.387 ns | 6.2x |
| Supply current | 0.677 mA | 0.621 mA | 3.786 mA | 6.1x |
| Threshold voltage shift | 0.842 mV | 0.780 mV | 4.231 mV | 5.4x |

**Condition:** early-warning mode, which uses the 0 hour and 24 hour readings
ONLY and never sees 96 hours. Test lots. LightGBM, rung 4 of the model ladder.
Source: `results/module_b.csv`.

**Always give both columns.** Error on defective parts is 5.4 to 16 times worse
than on good parts. That is expected, because defective parts are precisely the
ones whose behaviour departs from the population the model learned. But a single
headline MAE is a misleading summary for a safety application.

For context, the naive baseline (draw a straight line through the first two
readings and extend it) gives 2.868 uA on Iddq. The full model is 4.7 times
better.

## 3. The conformal guarantee

*Conformal risk control* puts a proven ceiling on our **expected** escape rate.
You pick a target, called alpha, and the method guarantees the expected escape
rate stays at or below it.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| **The number** | The guarantee **holds at every alpha tested, from 1% to 20%**, with no statistically significant violation |
| **Condition** | 8 alpha values, 3 detectors, **40 independent calibration splits each, split by lot**. One-sided t-test of the hypothesis that the true escape rate exceeds alpha. All p-values above 0.33. Largest overshoot 0.4 standard errors. |
| **Source** | `results/conformal_summary.csv`, `results/conformal_significance.csv` |

The cost of the guarantee, which is the honest half of the story:

| Alpha (guaranteed expected escape rate) | Measured escape rate | Yield loss |
|---|---|---|
| 1% | 0.82% | 39.0% |
| 2% | 1.76% | 23.8% |
| 3% | 2.95% | 18.2% |
| 5% | 5.00% | 11.6% |
| 7.5% | 7.62% | 7.0% |
| 10% | 9.90% | 4.4% |
| 15% | 14.68% | 1.6% |
| 20% | 19.48% | 0.97% |

**Condition:** the fused Module A detector. Calibration and evaluation both drawn
from validation and test lots only, never training lots.

**Say "expected".** The bound is on the average over future parts. Individual
calibration draws land above alpha 30% to 57% of the time, depending on alpha; that is
how an expectation behaves, and it is why we show 40 splits rather than one.

## 4. The cumulative ladder

Each rung includes everything below it. This is the build story.

| Rung | What it contains | Recall at 93% yield | 95% confidence interval | Escape rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C0 | Static datasheet limits alone | not applicable | | 100.0% |
| C1 | plus AEC-Q001 Dynamic PAT | 54.33% (232 of 427) | [49.6, 59.0] | 45.67% |
| C2 | plus trajectory features | 79.86% (341 of 427) | [75.8, 83.4] | 20.14% |
| C3 | plus robust multivariate | **92.74%** (396 of 427) | **[89.9, 94.8]** | 7.26% |
| C4 | plus unsupervised machine learning | 92.27% (394 of 427) | [89.3, 94.4] | 7.73% |

**Condition:** test lots, 7.00% yield loss at every rung, rungs fused by taking
the strongest normalised signal (normalised against training-lot parts, no
labels). Source: `results/ablation_cumulative.csv`.

**State the plateau explicitly.** C4 is 2 defective parts below C3, out of 427.
The confidence intervals overlap almost completely. The honest reading is
**the ladder plateaus at L3**, not that L4 makes things worse.

C0 has no threshold-free score because it is a fixed pass or fail rule. It
catches 0.0% of every injected defect type, which is the point: it is the
strawman.

## 7. The decision layer (new)

The six tiers an inspector actually sees, on every one of the 24,000 test parts:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Defects held (REVIEW or REJECT) | **92.0%** |
| Defects sent to re-test (fixture suspect or measurement invalid) | 1.2% |
| Real defects told "do not reject" as fixture suspect | **0** |

signature."*

# Section 2: The five findings that carry slides

## Finding 1: The derivative argument

**Claim.** The same argument works twice: for levels in Module A and for drift
rates in Module B. Limits must be relative to the batch, not to a fixed number.

**Evidence.** In Module A, static limits reject 358 good parts from a shifted
batch while dynamic limits reject 0. In Module B, using the datasheet limit as
the safety threshold is **structurally inert**: every injected defect is inside
the datasheet limits at every checkpoint by construction, so a predicted worst
case essentially never crosses an engineering limit. Measured, that rule catches
**0.9%** of defects. Swap the datasheet limit for a batch-derived safe limit,
built only from earlier lots, and the identical rule catches **16.2%**, at 0.25%
yield loss with 54.3% precision. Source: `results/safety_slopes_union.csv`.

**Slide line.** "Static limits fail on levels and fail again on drift rates. The
same fix works both times: compare each part to the batch it was burned in with."

## Finding 2: Isolation Forest catches 0% of the hardest defect class

**Evidence.** Isolation Forest catches **0.0% of 60 Type III parts** (all lots,
7% yield-loss operating point). Type III is the "centre-hider": normal on every
individual measurement, abnormal only in combination. Isolation Forest splits
one measurement at a time, so it cannot isolate a point that is central on every
axis. This contradicts the ITC 2020 industrial benchmark, where Isolation Forest
is among the strongest methods; we report the contradiction rather than reorder
the table, and our dataset deliberately over-weights the centre-hider class.

It is not a feature-count problem: on a narrow 20-feature level-only matrix
Isolation Forest gets **42.2%** overall recall at 93% yield, against **67.7%** on
the wide 83-feature matrix. Source: `results/isoforest_feature_width.csv`.

**Slide line.** "Isolation Forest finds none of the centre-hiders. An
axis-by-axis method cannot isolate a part that looks normal on every axis."

## Finding 3: A five-parameter robust statistic beats the ML methods

**Claim.** On the class that needs correlation reasoning, a five-parameter
statistical method beat every machine-learning method by a wide margin.

**Evidence.** On mild Type IV (correlation break, the hardest tier, 141 parts,
all lots, 7% yield loss): Mahalanobis distance with robust (MCD) covariance
catches **90.1%**, 95% confidence interval **[84.0, 94.0]**. The best ML method,
Local Outlier Factor (a density method, not a neural network), catches
**36.2%** [28.7, 44.4]. The neural autoencoder catches **30.5%** [23.5, 38.5].
Isolation Forest catches 2.1%. The intervals do not come close to overlapping.
Source: `results/per_type.csv`.

**Slide line.** "For the defect that hides in the correlations, classical robust
statistics beat the best ML method by 54 points and the neural network by 60. We
report what worked, not what sounds modern."

## Finding 4: Two attribution methods disagree, and that argues for the simple one

**Evidence.** Rank correlation between the SHAP importance ordering (LightGBM)
and an equally accurate Huber regression is **Spearman rho = 0.617, p = 0.077**,
not significant. SHAP ranks the 24 hour reading first; Huber ranks the 0 hour
reading first. Diagnosis: the two readings are **0.987 correlated**, and removing
the 0 hour reading from the model changes test error from 0.6103 to 0.6094 uA,
which is nothing. When two inputs are near-duplicates, the credit split between
them is a property of the model, not the physics. Source:
`results/explainability_metrics.json` (`attribution`),
`results/attribution_comparison.csv`, generated by `run_explainability.py`.

**Slide line.** "When two equally accurate models disagree about why, the
explanation you show an inspector should be the one they can recompute by hand."

## Finding 5: Static limits scrap 358 good parts over a process shift

**Evidence.** 3,000 components from six batches where the process shifted as a
whole. At the AEC-Q001 standard 6 sigma multiplier, static limits reject **358**
and dynamic limits reject **0**. All-lots figure, see Section 1 item 6.

**Slide line.** "One process excursion, 358 good components scrapped by fixed
limits and zero by adaptive ones."

# Section 5: Judge question answers

**1. Where will the data come from?** A synthetic generator grounded in the
Arrhenius model, AF 77.66 at Ea = 0.7 eV, so 168 hours equals 1.488 field years.
Production deployment needs representative data from SAC, which we state as a
pilot dependency.

**2. How do you handle so few defect labels?** Every unsupervised detector is
fitted on known-good parts from training lots only. We checked whether that clean
reference does the work: fitting on contaminated data instead moves Isolation
Forest from 67.7% to 74.0%, so the assumption is not load-bearing.
[`results/all_methods.csv`]

**3. Why not deep learning?** Four timepoints, tabular data, and explainability
is scored. On the hardest defect tier a five-parameter robust statistic beat the
neural autoencoder 90.1% to 30.5%. [`results/per_type.csv`]

**4. How do you avoid false alarms?** Batch-relative baselines, six decision
tiers, a published yield-loss curve, and a spatial rule that blames the oven only
when the part carries the oven's thermal signature. Measured on test lots: the
tiers hold **1.7%** of good parts for review; 6.7% get something other than a
plain PASS, mostly WATCH (release and monitor). The trap classes built to fool
us are fully passed 95.0% (Type VI) and 51.7% (Type Va) of the time.

**5. How do you know your flagged parts are actually defective?** On synthetic
data we know by construction, which is why we built traps that punish
over-flagging. On real data we would confirm flagged parts by extended HTOL.

**6. How do you validate across new lots?** Lot-grouped chronological splits:
train on lots 0 to 143, validate on 144 to 191, test on 192 to 239. Leave-one-
lot-out agrees (autoencoder 92.3% mean recall, sd 8.6).

**7. Can the model explain its decision?** Three layers, measured: reason
correctness 77% to 100% by defect class, stability under re-measurement 90.0%,
and counterfactuals that are validated against the whole measurement record.
Five example reports in `reports/`.

**8. How do you distinguish a sensor fault from a component fault?** A
data-quality gate runs before any model and routes incomplete records to
MEASUREMENT_INVALID. For chamber effects we use physics: heat makes a part leaky
and slow and lowers its threshold voltage, while process variation makes leaky
parts fast. A part is labelled FIXTURE_SUSPECT only if its board is clustered
AND its own shift since 0 h has that thermal sign. On test lots, 0 real defects
are excused this way.

**9. Does this replace engineering limits?** No. Datasheet limits stay mandatory
and our statistical limits are always clipped to them.

**10. What happens when the model is uncertain?** WATCH or REVIEW with the
prediction interval, or MEASUREMENT_INVALID and a re-test.

**11. Does it generalise to another parameter?** The pipeline is
parameter-agnostic; only the limit and degradation direction are configured per
parameter. Five parameters with different units, scales and distributions.

**12. What is your false negative rate?** Bounded in expectation by conformal
risk control, and validated: it holds at every alpha from 1% to 20% across 40
lot-grouped splits, all p above 0.33.

**13. Why does your ablation stop improving at L3?** Adding the ML rung changes
recall by 2 parts out of 427, inside the confidence intervals.

**14. Why are counterfactuals offered for only 7.9% of flagged parts?** A single
changed reading can only clear a part if that is the only checkpoint where the
rules fire, and a joint anomaly has no single reading to change at all: 11.8% of
parts flagged on one measurement get one, 0% of joint anomalies.

**15. Why is the Type Va trap only fully passed 52% of the time?** Va is an
elevated but healthy part, and the level rule and the joint-detector review
branch sometimes hold it. That is the measured cost of catching centre-hiders,
and we report it rather than tune it away.

**16. How do you know the synthetic defects are realistic?** They implement the
ITC 2020 finding that escaped defects sit near the centre of every parameter.
We also audit for shortcuts: we found and fixed two construction artifacts
(v1.0 smoothness, v1.1 values pinned to the spec margin) and each now has a
validator assertion. [`results/CHANGED_NUMBERS.md`]

# Section 6: Known weaknesses, stated plainly

1. **Validation is entirely synthetic.** *Answer:* "Correct, and we state it as a
   pilot dependency. The generator is Arrhenius-grounded, implements a published
   industrial finding, and has traps so the numbers are not self-flattering."
2. **Survivorship bias is measured but not fixed.** A survivors-only model
   under-predicts the true final Iddq of pulled parts by 30.9 uA, 62% of the
   datasheet limit; the datasheet-limit surrogate recovers only 0.6 uA. The fix
   is a censored-regression loss, scoped as next work.
3. **Type VI has zero parts in the test set.** Labelled all-lots; LOT146 is in
   validation.
4. **Type III has only 60 parts, 8 in the test lots.** Realistic rate, kept.
5. **Prediction intervals were miscalibrated before the conformal layer**:
   nominal 95% delivered 92.6% to 95.4% coverage.
6. **The Type Va trap costs yield.** See question 15.
7. **Conformal calibration excludes training lots entirely.** Conservative, by
   choice: exchangeability is the whole assumption.
8. **Layer 2 attribution is model-dependent.** See Finding 4.
9. **Centre-hiders are only partly held by the deployed tiers (35% to REVIEW).**
   Lowering the joint threshold catches more at a direct cost in Va trap holds.
